Abstract.

Non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM) aims to disaggregate aggregate household electricity consumption into individual appliance usage and thus enables more effective energy management. While deep learning has advanced NILM, it remains limited by its dependence on labeled data, restricted generalization, and lack of explainability. This paper introduces the first prompt-based NILM framework that leverages large language models (LLMs) with in-context learning. We design and evaluate prompt strategies that integrate appliance features, timestamps and contextual information, as well as representative time-series examples on widely used open datasets. With optimized prompts, LLMs achieve competitive state detection accuracy and demonstrate robust generalization without the need for fine-tuning. LLMs also enhance explainability by providing clear, human-readable explanations for their predictions. Our results show that LLMs can reduce data requirements, improve adaptability, and provide transparent energy disaggregation in NILM applications.

Keywords: 
Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring, Large Language Models, Energy Disaggregation, Prompt Engineering

1. Introduction

Non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM) aims to infer the energy consumption of individual appliances by analyzing the total electricity usage, thus avoiding the need for sub-metering individual devices (Hart 1992). By providing detailed appliance-level energy consumption data, the NILM technique empowers users with actionable insights for energy savings, enhances smart home automation, and facilitates energy demand management (Zoha et al. 2012). However, mathematically, decomposing the aggregated signal into individual appliance contributions is an ill-posed problem (Hart 1992). This challenge requires powerful modeling approaches capable of learning intricate patterns from the data (Kolter and Johnson 2011; Makonin and Popowich 2015).

Deep learning (DL) (LeCun et al. 2015) has significantly advanced the field of NILM due to its ability to autonomously learn complex features and model intricate dependencies directly from raw aggregate power signals (Kelly and Knottenbelt 2015; Du et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2018; Yue et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2022; Ayub and El-Alfy 2023; Yu et al. 2024b). Although DL-based NILM methods have demonstrated promising performance, further refinement and validation remain active research areas. The main limitations include data dependency, poor generalization, and lack of explainability. These limitations create a significant gap between the theoretical performance of NILM algorithms and their practical applicability in real-world scenarios (Xue et al. 2024).

Recently, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across a vast range of domains (Zhao et al. 2023), driven by advancements in natural language processing, reasoning, and particularly in-context learning (Dong et al. 2024). LLMs can perform specific tasks based on instructions and knowledge provided within a prompt, and often achieve reasonable performance in zero-shot or few-shot settings without task-specific fine-tuning (Wang et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2020). These unique strengths motivate investigating their applicability to NILM, leading us to focus on the following three compelling and opening research questions (RQs):

In this paper, we explore the above three RQs by conducting a systematic experimental study that examines the effectiveness and potential of modern LLMs when applied to NILM tasks using prompt-based inference. As Fig. 1 shows, we design and implement a unified framework (LLM4NILM) centered around advanced prompt engineering, and systematically integrate various prior knowledge such as appliance features, one-shot examples, timestamps, and contextual information from previous time windows. These meticulously crafted prompting strategies are then rigorously evaluated on the widely used REDD benchmark dataset (Kolter and Johnson 2011), allowing us to provide initial yet robust answers to the posed RQs. Our approach methodically tests foundational LLMs on core NILM objectives, appliance state, and power prediction, to map out their capabilities. Due to page limitations, a detailed discussion of related work is presented in Appendix C.

Through this structured exploration, our work establishes clear performance baselines for prompt-based LLMs in NILM and uncovers key enabling factors and challenges. For instance, our case studies on prompt components reveal that incorporating knowledge injection and contextual information significantly boosts performance, with contextual information improving the F1-score from 0.6479 to 0.7387 on the case study dataset. Furthermore, extensive testing against conventional DL baselines on the unseen REDD House 1 demonstrates that with optimized prompts, the DeepSeek-V3-0324 model can achieve an average F1-score of 0.676. These findings not only delineate effective pathways for leveraging LLMs but also highlight remaining obstacles such as output stability and inference latency, thereby paving the way for future research.

In summary, our main contributions are as follows:

  • •

    To the best of our knowledge, we conduct the first systematic study applying prompt-driven, general-purpose LLMs to core NILM tasks, and propose a comprehensive framework for the methodical exploration and evaluation of this novel approach on standard benchmarks.

  • •

    We demonstrate the effectiveness of our framework, showcasing that through optimized prompt engineering incorporating diverse knowledge sources, LLMs can adeptly perform core NILM objectives, achieving competitive appliance state detection accuracy.

  • •

    We establish that this LLM-based approach offers significant advantages in robust zero-shot generalization across diverse, unseen environments without task-specific fine-tuning, and enhances explainability by generating verifiable explanations for its disaggregation outputs.

2. Prompting LLMs for NILM: A Unified and Systematic Practice

LLMs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in in-context and zero-shot learning in other domains, which shows their potential suitability for NILM tasks. As mentioned in RQs, the core challenge is effectively conveying the domain knowledge required for NILM tasks via LLM through prompts. In this section, we first design the basic prompt strategy (Section 2.1) to ensure that the LLM can perform NILM tasks. Then we explore more advanced prompt strategies, including incorporating timestamps (Section 2.2) and contextual information (Section 2.3) for performance enhancement.

2.1. Foundational Prompts

For the design of foundational prompts for NILM, we draw upon established principles (Zhao et al. 2023) and structure our prompts with several key components detailed below: a Base Prompt defining the LLM’s role and the core task, Knowledge Injection providing domain-specific information, One-shot Examples to illustrate the task, and specifications for the Input Data.

Base Prompt (Role & Task). An appropriate prompting strategy can stimulate the LLM’s capabilities for specific tasks. Here, we inform the LLM of its role as an expert in the NILM domain. Nevertheless, we do not guide the model in performing chain-of-thought or reasoning. Due to the complexity of NILM tasks, reasoning may lead to incorrect inferences and consume more time. The task description is crucial for guiding the LLM’s understanding of the NILM task. We provide a clear and concise description of the task, including the goal of disaggregating total power consumption into individual appliance usage. Additionally, we specify the required input and output formats and the expected time window length for the task. It is worth noting that we set two tasks: state prediction and power prediction, which have different descriptions. The corresponding time window lengths may also differ for these tasks.

Knowledge Injection. We inject relevant knowledge about appliances to enhance the LLM’s understanding of the NILM domain. The most basic requirement is to inform the LLM about the types of appliances involved in the task. For example, in the REDD dataset, we provide a list of appliances, involving refrigerator, washing machine, microwave and dishwasher. In addition, we provide the power consumption characteristics of each appliance, which are extracted from historical data using statistical methods (e.g., standby power, ON power range, average ON duration, usage pattern, typical cycle duration, etc.).

One-shot Example. We provide several examples to help the LLM understand how to transform input data into output results. These examples include input data and corresponding output results. Intuitively, the more diverse the examples are regarding aggregated power consumption patterns, the better the model can learn from them. However, due to the token limit of API calls, we can only provide a limited number of examples. The examples also help the LLMs understand the format and task requirements.

Input Data. The input is the original aggregated power consumption data. We format the input and output data in JSON format, consistent with the NILM system’s approach (Xue et al. 2024). In this paper, we enable the API’s JSON mode and specify the corresponding format in the prompts.

The above four components constitute our foundational prompts for NILM. Through them, we can effectively guide the LLM in understanding the NILM task and provide necessary prior knowledge. The complete prompt can be found in Appendix A.1 and A.2.

2.2. Enhancing Prompts with Timestamps

In the design of foundational prompts, timestamps are not included by default, primarily because their inclusion incurs a substantial token overhead. However, temporal features are valuable for NILM tasks, as the state and power consumption of appliances are often time-dependent. For example, dishwashers are rarely used in the early morning, and refrigerators typically exhibit periodic power consumption patterns. Therefore, we explore the possibility of incorporating temporal features into the prompts. We include timestamp information (HH:mm:ss) in the prompts to help the LLM better understand appliance usage patterns. Specifically, we modify the base prompt, input data, and one-shot example in the foundational prompts. We add external explanations for temporal features and include a timestamp field in each input data entry. The changed prompt can refer to List  in Appendix A.1.

2.3. Enhancing Prompts with Context

When using LLMs for NILM, we typically rely on API calls for inference. However, simple API services do not provide contextual information. As a result, the inferences for different time windows are independent and previous results cannot be leveraged to inform subsequent ones. Yet, connections between time windows are inherent in NILM tasks. In traditional DL methods, researchers use filtering and other post-processing techniques to smooth the transitions between time windows. Our approach incorporates the inference results from the previous time window into the prompt to establish this connection. The modified prompt can refer to List  in Appendix A.1.

3. Evaluation

In this section, we first present case studies for prompt optimization through effectiveness analysis of different prompt components. We then validate the efficacy of optimized prompts using the large-scale REDD dataset and further access its generalization and explainability across different conditions. Detailed description of the dataset, preprocessing procedures, and evaluation metrics can be found in Appendix D.

3.1. Prompt Engineering with Case Studies

To assess the impact of different components of the prompt and strive to develop the optimal prompt for achieving the best NILM performance, we initially conduct case studies using a smaller dataset. We select one-day data within the REDD dataset for the case studies, during which four appliances were activated. In each round of testing, 100 data points are uploaded (window_length=100).

According to Section 2, we evaluate the effectiveness of each component of the foundational prompt. Role & Task and Input Data are the most fundamental components of the foundational prompt (Base). We gradually add the One-Shot Example and Knowledge Injection components to evaluate their impact on the NILM task. Based on the foundational Prompt, we utilize timestamp and contextual information to enhance it. While evaluating performance, we also simultaneously assess the cost of adding corresponding information. We record the consumption of relevant tokens and the expenses incurred from using the API. Table 1 presents the F1-score metrics for different prompts under the DeepSeek-V3-0324 model, along with its associated token usage and costs.

From Table 1, we observe that adding One-Shot Example, Knowledge Injection, Timestamps, and Context Information can all enhance the model’s performance. The improvements from Knowledge Injection and Context Information are the most significant, while the enhancement from adding timestamp information is relatively minor. Counter-intuitively, combining both Timestamp and Contextual information results in decreased performance compared to using Context alone. This indicates that the LLM may encounter difficulties in simultaneously processing and prioritizing multiple complex cues within the current prompt structure, potentially due to information overload or conflicting signals between absolute time and recent state history. Although adding components typically increases token usage and cost, the findings demonstrate that base prompt with one-shot example, knowledge injection and contextual information provides the most favorable performance-to-cost trade-off among the evaluated enhancements. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the DeepSeek-V3-0324 model, when utilizing this optimized prompt configuration, effectively identifies the operational status for most of the monitored appliances.

In addition to state prediction, we also explore the feasibility of using the same prompt-based LLM approach for direct appliance power estimation. The whole prompts can be seen in Appendix A.3 and A.4. However, initial experiments reveal significant challenges in achieving accurate power value predictions solely through prompting. For instance, metrics like Signal Aggregate Error (SAE) indicate substantial discrepancies in total energy estimation. Specifically, the average SAE for LLM4NILM is approximately 0.425, whereas the Seq2Point baseline achieves a much lower SAE of around 0.170. These results highlight that accurately regressing precise, continuous power values and capturing the fine-grained dynamics of appliance consumption directly from aggregate signals remains a considerable difficulty for current LLMs when guided only by textual prompts and limited examples. Given these quantitative results, and acknowledging that determining the operational state is often the primary goal for many practical NILM applications (as average power consumption can frequently be inferred from the state), our subsequent large-scale experiments and primary analysis focus predominantly on the state detection task.

3.2. Performance Analysis of LLM4NILM

3.2.1. Effectiveness Analysis against Conventional DL Models (RQ1)

To demonstrate the efficacy of LLM4NILM, we conduct tests on a larger dataset. Data from REDD houses 2 and 3 serve as the source for LLM prompt knowledge and for training the DL baseline models. Data from house 1 is used exclusively as the common unseen test set for evaluating both the LLM and DL approaches.

For the training-based DL models, we use Seq2Seq and Seq2Point with CNNs (Zhang et al. 2018) as representative baseline models, as they are well-established and commonly benchmarked approaches in the NILM field. The models directly predict power consumption. Compared to the complex post-processing methods in other papers (Yu et al. 2024b), we only use the thresholds to obtain appliance state predictions.

For LLMs, we apply all the prompt optimization methods mentioned in Section 2. The window_length is set to 100. We test on DeepSeek-V3-0324, a large-scale model, and the more lightweight GPT-4.1-mini. We do not conduct large-scale experiments on the reasoning model, as it often engages in excessive reasoning without significant performance improvement. For instance, preliminary tests with a reasoning-focused model (DeepSeek-R1) reveal that it sometimes attempts to devise explicit mathematical formulae to determine appliance activation, rather than leveraging the provided examples and knowledge in the intended pattern-recognition manner. Such approaches do not align with our goal of evaluating prompt-based pattern interpretation for NILM.

A performance comparison between the LLM and DL methods can be found in Table 2. The LLM4NILM framework, utilizing the DeepSeek-V3-0324, and the Seq2Point model both achieve the highest overall average F1-score of 0.676. For DeepSeek-V3, the disaggregation performance for the refrigerator is less effective, with an F1-score of 0.534. This is lower than its score for the washing machine and also significantly below the 0.778 achieved by the Seq2Point model for the refrigerator. This reduced performance on the refrigerator may be attributed to its continuous and cyclical energy consumption characteristics, which differ from the more event-based signatures of other appliances.

3.2.2. Generalization across Appliances and Environments (RQ2)

We investigate whether LLMs can achieve generalization across diverse environments without task-specific fine-tuning. A key aspect of our evaluation involved utilizing appliance statistical features in the prompts (List ) that were extracted from the data of REDD House 2 and 3. The LLM’s performance was then tested on REDD House 1, an entirely unseen environment for the model during the prompt engineering phase, providing a direct testbed for cross-household generalization. Furthermore, our proposed framework is designed for adaptability. New appliance types or different combinations of appliances can be handled by simply modifying the corresponding prompt requirements and injecting the relevant knowledge. This ability to generalize across different appliances and environments by merely adjusting prompts stands in contrast to conventional DL methods, which often require cumbersome transfer learning or complete retraining processes when encountering new appliances or adapting to new environmental conditions.

3.2.3. Enhancing Explainability for Energy Disaggregation (RQ3)

We examine whether LLMs can enhance the explainability of the NILM process by providing several justifications for each appliance. Our methodology involves selecting several representative time-series snippets of aggregate power. For these snippets, we prompt the LLM not only to perform disaggregation but also to generate textual explanations for its decisions regarding the ON/OFF status of each appliance (Appendix B). These generated explanations are then compared against the ground truth appliance states and the characteristics of the aggregate power signal.

We find that, in most instances, the LLMs are capable of generating plausible explanations. These explanations often correctly identify specific patterns in the aggregate signal, such as sudden power changes, or correlate the observed data with the known characteristics of appliances (e.g., typical power draw, operational cycles) that are provided as prior knowledge in the prompts. While LLMs are themselves complex deep neural networks, their potential for enhanced explainability in this context stems from their inherent ability to generate human-readable text that can serve as a rationale for their outputs. These explanations can offer valuable insights into the model’s decision-making process. Moreover, even when the disaggregation output is not entirely accurate, the explanation provided can sometimes reveal misunderstandings or misinterpretations by the LLM, thereby offering clues for refining the prompts or the injected knowledge to improve future performance.

4. Discussion

In Section 3, we demonstrated the effectiveness of LLMs in NILM tasks. However, the practical application of LLMs in real-world systems still faces several challenges. In this section, we discuss these challenges and propose potential solutions.

Output Stability and Formatting. While LLMs demonstrate robust performance, we occasionally observe minor deviations from strict output formats, such as slight sequence length mismatches. Reducing the window size can mitigate these issues but may compromise the model’s ability to capture longer-term dependencies. Fortunately, such discrepancies have minimal impact on evaluation metrics after straightforward post-processing. Improving LLMs’ capacity to consistently adhere to complex structural and length constraints remains an important direction for future research (Liu et al. 2024).

Inference Latency and Cost. Relying on API-based LLMs introduces latency and computational costs that pose challenges for real-time NILM applications. Although inference costs per query may surpass those of conventional DL models, these are offset by significantly reduced requirements for large-scale data labeling and extensive model training. Moreover, rapid advances in model compression (Stojkovic et al. 2024), efficient attention mechanisms, and hardware acceleration are expected to further decrease both latency and cost, making practical LLM deployment increasingly attainable.

Data Privacy and Edge Deployment. Transmitting fine-grained household energy data to external servers raises substantial privacy concerns. A promising solution is to deploy NILM directly on edge devices, such as smart meters or gateways. While running large LLMs on resource-constrained hardware remains a challenge, recent progress in compact open-source models, coupled with fine-tuning and model compression techniques, is making on-device LLM-based NILM increasingly feasible. Edge deployment not only safeguards data privacy but also reduces latency and enables more personalized, real-time energy management (Zhang et al. 2024b; Rao et al. 2024; Yu et al. 2024a).

5. Conclusion

In this paper, we present a systematic investigation into applying prompt-based, general-purpose LLMs to core NILM tasks. Through the exploration of various prompt engineering strategies and evaluation on established benchmarks, our findings demonstrate that LLMs, guided by carefully crafted prompts, can achieve competitive disaggregation accuracy. This approach facilitates strong zero-shot generalization across diverse households without requiring task-specific fine-tuning, and offers enhanced explainability for NILM systems. Our work underscores the significant potential of leveraging the in-context learning and reasoning capabilities of LLMs via prompt engineering, thereby charting a promising new direction for future research and application in the field of energy disaggregation.

Appendix C Related Work

In recent years, DL approaches commonly incorporate artificial neural network components such as CNNs (LeCun et al. 1998), RNNs (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997), and Transformers (Vaswani et al. 2017; Yu et al. 2024b), which have consistently enabled state-of-the-art performance on public energy disaggregation datasets. All of these methods typically require large amounts of labeled training data, which is expensive and difficult to acquire at scale (Rafiq et al. 2024; Kelly and Knottenbelt 2015). Models trained on one set of homes or appliances might perform poorly when deployed in new environments, often requiring retraining or complex transfer learning strategies (Wang et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2022). Moreover, the internal decision-making processes of many conventional DL models can be opaque, posing challenges for understanding their predictions and building trust (Mollel et al. 2022). While LLMs are also complex neural networks, their capacity to generate textual justifications for their outputs, as explored in our work, offers a distinct avenue for achieving a degree of verifiable explainability.

LLMs have recently demonstrated promising capabilities in related time-series analysis domains such as forecasting and classification (Xue and Salim 2023; Gruver et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024a). However, their specific effectiveness in tackling the unique signal decomposition challenges inherent to NILM via prompting has not been established. Our work serves as a pioneer in exploring how LLMs can be adapted for energy disaggregation.

Appendix D Experimental Setup Details

D.1. Dataset and Preprocessing

This paper uses the most widely used public benchmark, the REDD dataset. REDD provides energy consumption recordings from 6 residential houses in the USA and includes both high and low frequency active power measurements for whole-house mains and individual appliances.

For preprocessing, we utilize the low-frequency active power data from selected houses in the REDD dataset. We follow the established preprocessing practices, which involve computing aggregate power from mains, aligning timestamps, and resampling all series to a uniform 6-second interval via mean aggregation, followed by handling missing values. Ground truth appliance states (O​N=1,O​F​F=0ON=1,OFF=0) are generated using fixed power thresholds: Microwave (200W), Fridge (50W), Dishwasher (10W), and Washing Machine (20W).

D.2. Metrics

The performance of the appliance state detection task is evaluated using standard classification metrics. The evaluation is based on the counts of True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP), and False Negatives (FN) calculated over the evaluation period.

While accuracy is intuitive, it can be misleading in NILM tasks. This is due to class imbalance, where the OFF state significantly dominates the ON state. Therefore, metrics that focus on the detection of the minority (ON) state are often more informative.

To provide a single, balanced assessment, especially when dealing with imbalanced datasets like those in appliance state detection, the F1-score is commonly used. It is the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, giving a better measure of the incorrectly classified cases than accuracy does. The F1-score is particularly useful for evaluating the performance concerning the minority ON state.
